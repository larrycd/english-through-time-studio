    Measured accuracy

    Every tenth verse of the four Gospels was kept out of everything the translator learned. Each figure is the word overlap (F1, 0–100) between the studio's translation and the real translation of that verse; the baseline is what copying the input unchanged would score.
    These are honest, rough figures: overlap of words, not a judgement of meaning or style. Old English is measured against the Wessex Gospels (c. 1175), whose late spellings differ from the classical dictionary forms the studio writes, so its scores understate how readable the output is — and overstate nothing.

    The letters

    þ Þthorn: "th" (both sounds), Old English to the 1400s; later printed as y in "ye" (= the)
    ð Ðeth: "th", Old English, used interchangeably with þ
    ƿ Ƿwynn: "w", from the runic letter ᚹ; modern editions print w
    ȝ Ȝyogh: Middle English "gh" (night = niȝt) and "y" (ȝe = ye)
    æ Æash: the vowel of "cat"
    ſlong s: printed at the start and inside words until about 1800; round s at the end
    ⁊Tironian et: "and" (Old English "and" / "ond")
    ā ċ ġeditors' marks: long vowels, and c / g said "ch" / "y"; the scribes did not write them
    ᚠᚢᚦᚩᚱᚳfuthorc: the Anglo-Saxon runes, named after their first six letters

    Reading your file back

    Every SVG is written from the letter outlines, so the studio can read it back exactly. Saved PNG and JPEG files are read by matching the letter shapes; the checks above show both, live, for the text on screen.

    Dictionary

    Old spellings are matched loosely: ð = þ, ƿ = w, macrons and dots are ignored; in Middle English y = i, u = v, ȝ = y, and doubled letters count once. Inflected forms are found too (heofonum → heofon).

    About

    English Through Time Studio by Larry Dalton translates between today's English and three older stages of the language, writes them in period scripts, reads its own pictures back, looks words up and says them aloud. Everything runs on this computer at http://localhost:8772/; nothing is sent anywhere.

    How the translator works

    Modern English is tagged word by word (part of speech, subject or object, tense, person). Grammar words come from hand-made tables for each period (pronouns in every case, "the" in every gender and case for Old English, verb endings, helping verbs). Other words come from the Old and Middle English dictionaries, ranked by how often real translators chose them: the studio lined up 12,000 Wycliffe verses, 12,000 King James verses and 1,817 Old English Gospel verses with a modern translation and counted which words correspond. Old English nouns, verbs and adjectives are then put in the right case, number and person from the dictionary's own tables of forms. Going back to modern English, the studio finds the dictionary entry for each form (heofonum → heofon "heaven", dative plural) and puts the meaning in the right modern form. Word order is kept; idioms are not rewritten. Every word shows how it was chosen, and words that could not be found are kept and marked.

    Pronunciation

    Old and Middle English use the Wiktionary transcription when there is one for that exact word, and otherwise the studio's spelling rules for the period (Old English as around 1000; Chaucer's London around 1390). Early Modern English starts from eSpeak NG's British reading of the word and applies the main differences scholars reconstruct for around 1600 (r sounded after vowels, the vowels of "time" and "house" starting near "uh", "meat" with a long e, "name" with a plain long e). All of these are reconstructions; nobody recorded these voices. Speech is made by eSpeak NG from the IPA: a Swedish voice is used for Old and Middle English because it has the sounds that English has lost (the rolled r, the "ch" of loch, the ü of Old English y); the British voice for the later periods.
